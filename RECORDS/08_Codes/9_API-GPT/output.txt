## Charles Darwin
Charles Darwin (1809-1882) stands as one of the most influential figures in scientific history, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of life on Earth. His seminal five-year voyage aboard HMS Beagle (1831-1836) provided the crucial observational data and intellectual impetus, particularly from the Galápagos Islands, for his groundbreaking theories. Culminating in the publication of *On the Origin of Species* in 1859, Darwin meticulously articulated the theory of evolution by natural selection, positing that all species share common ancestors and diversify over vast timescales through a process where advantageous heritable traits become more prevalent in successive generations due to environmental pressures. Darwin's work not only provided a coherent, evidence-based mechanism for biodiversity but also revolutionized biology, challenging anthropocentric views and laying the foundational framework for modern evolutionary science, genetics, and ecology.

## Gregor Mendel
Gregor Mendel, an Austrian Augustinian friar and scientist, is widely acknowledged as the "Father of modern genetics" for his foundational work on heredity. Conducting meticulous hybridization experiments with thousands of pea plants (*Pisum sativum*) in his monastery garden, Mendel quantitatively analyzed the inheritance patterns of specific traits. This rigorous approach led him to deduce the existence of discrete hereditary "factors" (now known as genes) and formulate the fundamental laws of inheritance: the Law of Segregation, the Law of Independent Assortment, and the concept of Dominance. Although his seminal paper, "Experiments on Plant Hybridization" (1866), was largely overlooked during his lifetime, its rediscovery in 1900 revolutionized biology, establishing the indispensable groundwork for understanding biological inheritance and providing a crucial mechanism for evolutionary theory.

## Marie Curie
Marie Curie, born Maria Skłodowska, was a pioneering physicist and chemist whose groundbreaking research fundamentally transformed scientific understanding of matter and energy. A Polish and naturalized French citizen, Curie is renowned for her revolutionary work on radioactivity, a term she coined. Alongside her husband Pierre, she isolated and characterized two new elements, polonium and radium, laying the groundwork for the field of nuclear physics. Her meticulous experimental techniques not only confirmed the existence of these highly radioactive substances but also advanced the methodology for studying atomic phenomena. Curie's unparalleled achievements earned her two Nobel Prizes—one in Physics (1903) and one in Chemistry (1911)—making her the first woman to receive the award, the only person to win in two different scientific fields, and the only woman to win multiple Nobels. Beyond fundamental discovery, her work had profound practical applications, notably in medical diagnostics (e.g., mobile X-ray units during WWI) and cancer therapy. Despite facing significant societal and scientific barriers, Curie's unwavering dedication to scientific inquiry continues to inspire, solidifying her legacy as one of history's most influential scientists.

## Isaac Newton
Sir Isaac Newton (1642-1727) was a pivotal figure in the scientific revolution, whose groundbreaking work at Cambridge University fundamentally reshaped natural philosophy. His monumental *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) introduced the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, unifying celestial and terrestrial mechanics and establishing the foundation for classical physics. Concurrently, Newton developed infinitesimal calculus (fluxions), a powerful mathematical tool crucial for understanding change and motion. Beyond these, his extensive work in optics elucidated the composition of white light and led to the construction of the reflecting telescope. Newton's rigorous application of mathematical principles to physical phenomena established a new scientific paradigm that dominated for centuries, profoundly influencing subsequent scientific inquiry and the Enlightenment.

## Albert Einstein
Albert Einstein (1879-1955) stands as one of the most influential physicists of all time, whose groundbreaking theories fundamentally reshaped our understanding of the universe. His "miracle year" of 1905 saw the publication of papers on the photoelectric effect, for which he received the 1921 Nobel Prize, Brownian motion, and the Special Theory of Relativity, introducing concepts of space-time, mass-energy equivalence ($E=mc^2$), and the relativity of simultaneity. This was further expanded by his General Theory of Relativity (1915), which redefined gravity as a curvature of spacetime and became a cornerstone of modern cosmology. Beyond his prodigious scientific output, Einstein's life was marked by his advocacy for peace and social justice, leading him to flee Nazi Germany and become a prominent intellectual voice in the United States. His intellectual legacy continues to inspire scientific inquiry and philosophical debate, cementing his status as an icon of scientific genius and humanistic thought.

## Nikola Tesla
Nikola Tesla (1856–1943), a Serbian-American inventor and electrical engineer, was a prodigious visionary whose innovations fundamentally reshaped the modern world's electrical infrastructure. His work on alternating current (AC) systems, including the polyphase motor and the AC generator, directly challenged and ultimately triumphed over Edison's direct current (DC) in the 'War of the Currents,' enabling the efficient long-distance transmission of electricity and the widespread adoption of electrical power grids, notably demonstrated by the Niagara Falls hydroelectric plant. Beyond AC, Tesla's prolific output included the invention of the Tesla coil, foundational to radio technology and wireless communication research, as well as pioneering concepts in remote control and early explorations into X-rays. Though he faced financial struggles and some of his grandest visions remained unrealized during his lifetime, Tesla's profound contributions laid much of the groundwork for modern power systems, wireless communication, and technological advancement, securing his legacy as one of history's most influential engineers.
